## 2. What makes a topic work (the pattern behind the hits)
1. **A brand every Indian already knows** (Zudio, Maruti, Amul, Campa, Bisleri, Coke, Asian Paints, Swiggy, PVR, ITC).
2. **A paradox you can see in daily life**, stated as a fact that sounds impossible: no website but beating everyone;
   dairy giant that sells no milk; two "different" cars that are the same car; a can that costs 2.5× the bottle;
   colour is free yet a company sells it for millions; a watch with no screen worth $10B.
3. **Hidden money/power behind the ordinary thing** (₹ numbers, margins, market share).
4. **The answer is a mechanism, not trivia**: three forces, three gates, a flywheel, a trap. The viewer leaves able to
   explain it to a friend. Shareable = "I finally understand why…".
5. News helps only as a door (Birla Opus 2 years in, Milky Mist IPO, Nvidia–Hugging Face), never as the whole video.
   A news recap with no paradox (Starlink, Amazon quick commerce, Zepto ads) lands around 45–80k.

Fails: a concept with no face/brand, a foreign brand Indians don't use, praising ads, and "explaining technology"
for its own sake. **For Designomics: a design object + a famous Indian (or India-used) brand + a money paradox.**
Design topics already proven on his channel: Pantone (colour), Whoop (removing the screen), Coke can vs bottle
(pack size), Toyota/Maruti (badge engineering), Dyson (designing the boring motor), Wimbledon (invisible logos).

## 3. Title formula
- `Why <famous name> <does something that sounds wrong>?` — "Why Toyota Sells Maruti Cars Instead of Making Its Own".
- `How <name> <result with ₹/$ number> <without the obvious thing>` — "How Milky Mist Built a ₹11,000 Crore Dairy
  Business Without Selling Milk".
- `<Free/obvious thing>. So why <surprising money>?` — "Colour Is Free. So Why Do Brands Pay This Company Millions?"
- The viewer must NOT be able to guess the answer. Name a real brand. Avoid "Marketing Case Study" style titles about
  a campaign; avoid abstract nouns ("The Subscription Trap") unless a famous brand is in the title.

## 4. Anatomy of his script (HIS order; for us it is a toolbox, not a template)
Siddhesh, 2026-10-19: our scripts must not feel formula-like. Use these moves where the story needs them, change the
order, skip what doesn't serve this episode, and never reuse the same stock phrase every video.
Word budget: he speaks ~205 words/min; 12–14 min = 2,500–2,900 words. Siddhesh speaks slower (plan ~150 wpm):
7 min ≈ 1,050 words, 10 min ≈ 1,500, 12 min ≈ 1,800.

| Beat | Share | What happens | His devices |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Cold open | first 60–90 s | A concrete scene or checkable fact, then 3–5 escalating facts/numbers, then the contradiction | "If you search X right now…", "I want you to look at this bill", "Park a Baleno next to a Glanza…", "Do you know India is the largest producer of milk…" |
| 2. The question | 10–20 s | "So the big question is…" + often "I want to talk about three things" (the promise) | names the 2–3 questions the video will answer |
| 3. Sign-on | 5 s | "My name is Anurag Bansal and let's decode." (+ sponsor credit) | same line every video = ritual |
| 4. Primer | ~15 % | "Before we get into X, we first need to understand Y" — how the system normally works | one everyday analogy (paint's 3 gates, vegetable market for liquidity, torch for satellite coverage, table + cupboard for RAM/storage, car insurance for options, 10 shops in one mall for IPL revenue pool) |
| 5. Body: 3 parts | ~55 % | "The first reason/force/gate…", "that was the first… now the second" | each part ends on a mini-reveal; "But here's the thing / here's where it gets interesting" every 60–90 s |
| 6. Counter + honesty | inside body | "Now I know some of you might be thinking…" then answers it; caveats ("company estimate, take with a pinch of salt", "these are allegations") | builds trust; never oversells |
| 7. Callback | near end | returns to the opening image/number ("remember the ₹35,000 difference?") so the cold open pays off | closes the loop |
| 8. Zoom-out line | 1–2 sentences | one quotable lesson | "Money can buy an entry; only profitable repeat demand builds a moat." / "Fame opens the door; product and focus decide if people stay." |
| 9. Ending | 15–30 s | a personal question for comments + subscribe; no outro music | "Do you think…? Let me know." |

## 5. Writing devices to steal (these are the craft)
- **Named imaginary person + rupee math**: Harshita buys an ₹80,000 iPhone and asks Siri 10 things a day → $11/yr →
  × 1B users = $11B. Meera in Pune, 10 lakh notes × 50 paise = ₹5 lakh/month. Suresh the kirana owner, Rahul the
  electrical-shop owner, Rohit the RCB fan. Abstract economics becomes a person you can picture.
- **"Let's assume you run a…" thought experiment**: you run a hair-dryer company, a cafe paying ₹2 lakh rent (₹400 per
  open hour), a cola brand. The viewer makes the decision and hits the wall themselves.
- **Translate every number into an Indian ruler**: Nvidia's quarter > TCS+Infosys+HCL+Wipro in a year; Apple > India's
  GDP; Saudi PIF = 2× India's budget; a machine "costs more than a flat in Bombay"; $ always also in ₹.
- **Repeat the impossible line**: "I'll repeat myself: Milky Mist does not sell milk."
- **Second person, present tense, questions to the viewer**: "think about it", "you only tell me", "be honest".
- **Signposting** so a 13-minute video never feels lost: "three things", "first… second… third", "remember this,
  it'll matter later".
- **Personal proof, used rarely**: "I drive a Baleno", "I asked 30–40 Apple Watch users", "my wife's bag business".
- **Real clips as evidence** in the cold open (news anchors, a CEO quote, an old ad), then him.
- **Fairness**: shows the other side (high MRP also keeps offline shops alive; subscriptions also help students).
- **Sponsor bridge**: the topic's last sentence leads into the sponsor ("managing inventory is the real challenge…
  that's where Odoo…"), placed at ~35–45 % of the video, then "Now, back to…".

## 6. What NOT to copy
- His filler: "very very", "ladies and gentlemen", "honestly", "basically", "all that said and done", "I'll explain".
  Early 2025 videos are full of it; his best 2026 scripts have less. Siddhesh's script: cut these on the CHECK step.
- Over-long primers (NSE 21 min, Starlink 21 min): one analogy per idea, then move.
- Ending on a summary of the three points PLUS a lesson PLUS a question: pick the lesson + one question.
- Generic marketing-advice tangents ("if you are an entrepreneur, learn from this…").

## 7. How this becomes a Designomics script (rules for me)
1. Topic test before writing: famous brand Indians use? a design object you can SHOW? a paradox stated as a fact?
   a money number? a mechanism as the answer? Fewer than 4 yes → pick another topic.
2. Angle = his edge: Anurag explains the business; Siddhesh explains **why the design decision made that business**
   (the shape, colour, screen, button, pack, logo) and shows it as a designer would (before/after, markup, grid).
3. Length: aim 8–10 min (1,200–1,500 words at his pace). The data says 11–15 min is best for Anurag, but a new voice
   earns length; 7–8 min early episodes, grow to 10–12 once retention is known.
4. Two files in `/Volumes/T7 Shield/ReelEngine/projects/<slug>/`: `script.md` (from
   brain/templates/longform-script.md: ONLY what he says, plain spoken paragraphs) and `editor-brief.md` (from
   brain/templates/longform-editor-brief.md: what the video is about, the look, moment-by-moment "the viewer should
   get / on screen / him on or off camera", sources with links, things to find, watch-outs, posting kit). Spoken English with Indian units (lakh, crore, ₹ first); short sentences; one analogy per idea; a
   named person for every piece of maths. **"My take" is written in full in his voice** (opinion + what a designer
   learns + one honest doubt, 120–180 words); he tweaks, he never has to write it. Never invent his experiences.
4b. Relevance: the title and the door are about NOW (a 2026 event, dated in the file's "Why now" row). An older
   story is only allowed through a current door.
5. Every cold open passes: concrete in the first sentence, a number by sentence 3, the contradiction by 45 s,
   "the big question" by 60 s.
6. Overlap check — he has ALREADY covered: Pantone, Dyson, Whoop, Coke cans, Toyota badge engineering, Zomato→Eternal,
   Wimbledon logos, Ferrari EV + Jaguar's EV problem, Tupperware (inside the phone-repair video), Tropicana and Jaguar
   minimal logos (inside the luxury video), Cadbury Dairy Milk vs 5 Star, Fevicol ads, Nike, MRF stickers. Pick a
   different story or a clearly new angle.
7. His verdict 2026-10-19 overrides an "India first" reading of the data: he wants global design-business drama
   (Adobe–Figma, Apple v Samsung, Cadbury purple, Netflix font, Jaguar, OpenAI–io). Still give foreign stories ONE line
   that lands for an Indian viewer (a tool they use, an Indian parallel), but never pick a topic just because it is Indian.
8. **Curiosity gap test (his, 2026-10-19)**: (a) is it from the last few months, or does a current event reopen it?
   (b) can the viewer guess the answer from the title? If yes, reject. (c) does the title state a strange RESULT and
   hide the HOW? Never put the answer in the title. (d) is there already a deep video on it? Run
   `.venv/bin/yt-dlp --flat-playlist --print "%(view_count)s | %(title)s" "ytsearch8:<topic>"`; only small news clips
   = open lane. (e) evergreen "everyone knows" stories (Parle-G pack, Amul girl, Paytm box) are rejected even if they
   pass the topic test.

## Title rule (round 3, his verdict 2026-10-19) — overrides the news-style titles of round 2
- News-report titles failed ("Tesla took deposits, people finally saw the car" = "ok, that happened"). The title
  promises a HIDDEN TRUTH about something the viewer already uses or cares about; the news is why we make it this
  week and how the video opens, never what the title reports. No dates in titles.
- Learned from Enrico Tartarotti (UI/tech design, hits: "The Insane Design Of Messaging" 1.6M, "Short Form Content Is
  BROKEN" 1.5M, "The LIES That Make Your Tech ACTUALLY Work" 1.1M, "Who Is REALLY Making Billions From ChatGPT" 950k):
  everyday thing + hidden layer + "you/your" + one emotion word (ridiculous, insane, weird, lies, secrets, obsessed),
  repeatable series shapes ("The Ridiculous Engineering Of ___"). His misses = narrow news and personal topics.
- From Anurag: a known brand + a paradox that touches your money.
- OUR LANE: why the things you buy and use look/feel/behave the way they do, and who gets rich from that design
  decision. Enrico = how it works inside; Anurag = company strategy; us = the design decision as a money weapon.
- Our repeatable shapes: "The Sneaky Design Of ___", "Why Every ___ Looks The Same", "The Secret Business Of ___",
  "Why You ___". Test: would a non-designer feel "wait, I see that every day and never knew"?

## Title rule (round 4, his verdict 2026-10-19) — the balance
- Round 3 went too broad ("Why Companies Make You Wait" = no name, no hype, no views). A title needs BOTH: a famous
  name people already search for (Cybertruck, Netflix, Figma, Cadbury) AND a hidden reason they never knew.
  If a famous name is inside the video, it belongs in the title.
- Always give 3 title options per idea: specific (name + exact fact), mixed (name + hidden reason), broad (the
  concept), and mark one pick.
- Every idea must carry a real insight (a mechanism worth learning), not a news recap. If the story has no "why"
  (the Roadster was simply late, not a strategy), drop the angle and find the real one (the Cybertruck's steel).
- Idea briefs must be deep enough to script from: why now, the story in 4-6 beats with numbers, the insight, other
  cases (one Indian), the ending, what to check, opened sources. The ideas doc "Round 4" is the model.

## Density rule (his verdict 2026-10-19, after the Cybertruck idea)
- "In that video I'd get one insight: the steel isn't bendable. I'd be frustrated wasting seven minutes." An episode
  needs AT LEAST 8 separate insights, each a surprise on its own, all tied together by one story. Research until
  you have them (10+ searches, opened pages); if you can't reach 8, drop the idea instead of padding it.
- Every brief lists its insights numbered with a count in the heading. Model: the font brief in the ideas doc
  (11 insights: renting fonts, Netflix Sans, the disputed savings, Monotype's buying spree, private-equity owner,
  letter shapes not copyrightable, Arial, why Google Fonts is free, IKEA Verdanagate, the State Department font
  fight, everyone building their own).
- Nothing in the script that doesn't add an insight or move the story.
- Review lesson (font episode, 2026-10-19): when a hook number is disputed, never just say "might not be true" (it
  deflates the hook); state the dispute in one line and turn it into a bigger mechanism that keeps the stakes (one
  quote was small, the meter that never stops was not). Lists (acquisitions, timelines) are told as an image or
  action ("a big fish eating smaller fish"), max 2 named items + one callback name, the rest shown on screen.

## Spoken style (his verdict 2026-10-20: "it sounds like a script, AI generated, not a human")
Write it the way a YouTuber TALKS to camera, like Anurag (study his transcripts), not the way a writer writes:
- Talk TO the viewer: "You know,…", "See,…", "Now, I want you to think about this…", "right?", "Think about it.",
  "And the funny part is…", "Let me tell you…", "Now you might be wondering…", "Okay, now let's…", "Just remember this".
- Longer, joined sentences with "and", "so", "but"; the way people actually explain. Small natural repeats are fine.
- BANNED written-style tells: dramatic fragments ("Not the shows. Not the logo. The letters."), rule-of-three
  lists for rhythm, colon reveals, punchy epigram lines at the end of sections ("That's the power of whoever owns
  the letters."), poetic metaphors said in a literary way ("it crossed the sea"), perfectly balanced sentences.
  A metaphor is fine only said casually ("Basically, Monotype has been doing what a big fish does").
- Sign on like a person ("My name is Siddhesh, this is Designomics, and let's get into it"), sign off casually.
- Light filler is human, but cap it: "basically" / "honestly" / "literally" max ~3 each per video; never "very very".
- Spoken scripts run longer: ~2,000–2,200 words for 12–14 minutes; that's fine (his sweet spot is 11–15 min).
- Test: read each paragraph out loud as if explaining to a friend over chai. If it sounds like an article, rewrite.

## 7c. Story, not lecture (his rule, 2026-10-19)
- Start in a scene: a person, a place, a moment (a buyer in 2017 sending $50,000 for a car), not a definition.
- Someone to follow from start to end (a buyer, a founder, a designer, a company), with something to win or lose.
- Keep 2–3 questions open and pay each off later; never answer the title's question in the first half.
- Every explanation is told as what happened to someone ("so Tesla had a problem…"), with one picture-able example.
- A turn in the middle (what everyone believed was wrong) and a last reveal that changes how the opening looks.
- End on a line that lands (a twist, a callback, a strong opinion), then one question. No summary of points.
- Test: if a paragraph could sit in a textbook, rewrite it as something that happened.

## 7b. The Designomics episode formula (his idea, 2026-10-19)
The ingredients (in whatever order the story wants): news door (a strange 2026 RESULT with a number) · the named concept underneath (one analogy, one imaginary

## Case-study format + engagement (2026-10-23, his redirect from YouTube Studio's advice; overrides any "lecture" shape)
**Format = a case study about ONE recognisable company decision:** "Why did [company] do [visible thing]?"
- Topic source = what people already search: YouTube autocomplete (India) + his Studio Research tab. Free check:
  `curl "https://suggestqueries.google.com/complete/search?client=firefox&ds=yt&hl=en&gl=IN&q=why+maggi"`.
  An idea whose question already shows up in autocomplete has proven demand (e.g. "why hero splendor so popular",
  "why maggi was banned", "why maggi packet is white", "why louboutin shoes are so expensive", "why all cars look the
  same", "why did apple remove the sim card", "why did apple switch to aluminum" — all seen 23 Oct 2026).
- Every case study is built from VISUAL COMPARISONS: before/after, theirs/competitor, old/new, expected/actual. At
  least one side-by-side per section; the editor brief names each comparison.
- Shape (not a fixed order): the decision people noticed → what everyone assumes → what actually happened inside the
  company (the meeting, the people, the risk) → how the thing works (design/mechanics) → what it did to sales or
  costs → the lessons you can steal → ending callback.

**Engagement = character + humour, not just information.**
- The host is a CHARACTER: a designer who can't stop noticing things and is a bit obsessed, slightly sarcastic,
  self-aware, on the viewer's side ("companies are playing with us, and I'm going to snitch"). He has opinions.
- Humour devices (use 4–6 per episode, never forced):
  1. Re-enact the meeting: the executives as characters ("So the CEO walks in and says…"), he plays both sides.
  2. Indian everyday comparisons: mom, chai, Splendor uncle, the hostel friend, the shaadi relative, cricket.
  3. Absurd number rulers: "that's more than what IPL pays for…", "you could buy 4,000 Splendors with that."
  4. Self-roasting as a designer ("we designers will charge you ₹50,000 to move a logo 2 pixels").
  5. Fake-out: build the obvious answer, then "…yeah, no. That's not it."
  6. Callbacks: a running joke from the first minute that pays off at the end.
  7. Talk to the viewer: "Pause the video. Guess. … Wrong." / "Check your phone right now."
- Each joke marks a moment for the editor (meme / sound / reaction cut) in the editor brief.
- Still spoken English like Anurag, plain words; humour comes from situations, not puns.
